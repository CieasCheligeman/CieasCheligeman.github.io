This is not a prediction. Earthquake prediction — naming the time, place and size of a future event — is not possible with current science. What follows are descriptive rate statistics computed from the observed catalogue: a b-value from the Gutenberg–Richter relation, and expected aftershock counts from the generic Reasenberg–Jones model. “Once per 34 days” is a long-run average rate, not a statement about tomorrow.

安曇野 sits on a fault. The 糸魚川–静岡構造線 (Itoigawa–Shizuoka Tectonic Line, ISTL) runs north–south along the western edge of the 松本盆地, and its active fault zone passes directly beneath the city. It is one of the most active inland faults in Japan; the government’s Earthquake Research Committee models a magnitude 7.6 event on its central segment.

So the hazard that matters most here is usually not the distant subduction events filling this map — it is a shallow inland earthquake directly underfoot. The March 2011 長野県北部 earthquake (M6.7) and the 1998 上高地 swarm were both events of that inland kind.

The basemap is NASA GIBS Blue Marble shaded relief and bathymetry. The 日本海溝 (Japan Trench) and 南海トラフ (Nankai Trough) drop away exactly where the band of epicentres lies.

    The Moon and Sun lift the crust by a few centimetres, loading fault planes with stress on the order of 1–5 kPa — three orders of magnitude below the MPa-scale stress needed to rupture a fault.

    The effect is real, and extremely weak. Ide, Yabe & Tanaka (2016, Nature Geoscience) reported that large earthquakes fall slightly more often near peak tidal stress. The bias is a few percent, and it carries no power to predict an individual earthquake. This panel is a readout of physical quantities, not a warning. The positions are computed in the page from a truncated lunar and solar series, and were checked against NASA JPL Horizons for this site: lunar range within 16 km, altitude and azimuth within 0.08°.
